# 0014 - Valuation: turning sold comps into a number, and refusing to when they are not there

**Context:** Everything so far produces inputs to a question nobody has yet asked in code: what is this item worth, and is this listing a good deal? The pieces are now in place. Disappearance tracking produces `likely_sold` rows (1,001 of them, 956 usable after `0012`/`0013` filtering), each carrying the two separate scores `0007` established: `sale_confidence` (did a sale actually happen) and `price_confidence` (is the recorded price what a buyer handed over). Comp retrieval works through image hashing, `epid` and CLIP, filtered by the variant and spec fields. And measurement says the data is finally sufficient: sampling 100 active listings, 69% find three or more sold comps within their top-60 neighbours and the median is five. That number is what makes this stage buildable now rather than a month from now, and it is worth noting the earlier reading was wrong: counting only `epid`-keyed comp groups suggested just 14 products had two or more sales, because `epid` covers 13% of sold listings and the retrieval path covers far more.

**The thing that makes this stage dangerous rather than merely fiddly** is that every input is biased in the same direction and the bias compounds. `0003` established that a listing leaving the market means sold, **or** expired unsold, **or** withdrawn, and that `likely_sold` must be read as "left the market at roughly this asking price" and never as a sale price. `0005` established that Best Offer sales settle *below* asking. So a value estimate built from these comps is biased **high**, which means a deal score computed against it is biased **optimistic**: it will systematically say things are better deals than they are. That is exactly the wrong direction for a tool whose output is "you should buy this".

**Decision:** Add `ml/valuation.py` producing a `Valuation` for a listing: an estimated value, a comp count, an ordinal confidence, a signals breakdown, and a deal score. Four rules do the work.

**Sold comps only.** Valuation retrieves `status = 'likely_sold'` rows, never active ones. Active listings are asking prices, which is what `ml/match.py`'s `PriceContext` already reports and deliberately labels as such. Mixing the two would silently blend "what someone wanted" with "what the market cleared".

**Gate on sale confidence, weight by price confidence**, which is precisely the division of labour `0007` specified and this is the first code to use it. A comp below `MIN_SALE_CONFIDENCE` is *excluded*, because a relisted item that never sold is not weak evidence of a sale price, it is evidence of nothing. Comps that pass are then combined by a **weighted median** with `price_confidence` as the weight, so a Best Offer listing (price known to be an over-estimate) pulls less than a plain fixed-price sale. Median rather than mean throughout: the residual comp spread is 2.74x after `0013`, so outliers are still present and a mean would chase them.

**Delivered cost, not item price.** `Listing.total_cost` where shipping is known, falling back to `price` with the substitution recorded in signals. This matters most for exactly the cross-source comparison the project exists for, since a Facebook pickup has no shipping while an eBay comp does.

**Refuse to answer rather than guess.** Below `MIN_COMPS_FOR_VALUATION` usable comps, `estimated_value` is `None` and so is the deal score. 15% of listings currently have zero sold comps and a further 16% have only one or two; inventing a number for them would be worse than silence, because a confident-looking figure is acted on and a missing one is not. Confidence, when a valuation is produced, is ordinal (never a probability, same convention as `0005`) and derives from comp count, price spread among the comps, and their median sale confidence, with every contribution recorded in a signals dict so no number is opaque.

**Alternatives considered:** A mean, or a trimmed mean, was rejected for the outlier reason above. Weighting by CLIP similarity was rejected outright and this is the most important rejection here: `0009` measured that image similarity runs *anti*-correlated with comp quality, scoring 0.87 on useless prebuilt-PC neighbours against 0.77 on useful iPhone ones, so using it as a weight would systematically trust the worst comps most. Similarity gates *retrieval* and must never touch valuation. Correcting the upward bias with a fitted adjustment was rejected as unfounded: the size of the bias is unknown, it certainly varies by category and by whether Best Offer was enabled, and a made-up multiplier would convert a known unknown into a hidden one. It is surfaced as a caveat instead. Recency weighting was considered and deferred rather than rejected: the corpus is days old, so every comp is recent and a decay function would currently be untestable; revisit once there is a month of history, at which point it becomes genuinely important for hardware whose prices fall steadily. Including active listings as weak comps was rejected for the mixing reason above.

**Consequences:** Stage 4's headline number exists, and it is honest about when it cannot be computed, which will be roughly a third of listings at current comp density and should improve as sold history accumulates. Nothing is written to the database: valuation is computed on read, because the estimate changes as comps arrive and a stored value would be stale the moment it was written, unlike `sale_confidence` which is deliberately frozen at disappearance time because it describes a moment. The upward bias is the honest headline risk, and every valuation carries it as an explicit caveat rather than a footnote; the mitigation is that the tool reports what comps say and shows them, rather than issuing a verdict. `MIN_COMPS_FOR_VALUATION` and `MIN_SALE_CONFIDENCE` are settings because their right values are unknown and will change as the corpus grows. Finally, this closes the loop the project was built around: an item captured from Depop can now be identified against the eBay index, valued against eBay sold history, and scored, with the comps and their weaknesses shown alongside.
