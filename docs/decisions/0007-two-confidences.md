# 0007 - Splitting sale confidence from price confidence

**Context:** `0005` introduced a single `sale_confidence` score for a disappeared listing, combining every available signal multiplicatively. Reviewing what those numbers actually produce showed the score is answering two genuinely different questions at once and losing the distinction between them. **Did this listing result in a sale?** is informed by relist detection, auction bid counts, and whether the listing ran to its scheduled end. **Is the recorded price what the buyer actually paid?** is informed by whether the listing accepted Best Offers and whether the price is a mid-auction bid snapshot. Those are different uncertainties with different consequences, and collapsing them means stage 4 cannot tell a listing that probably never sold from one that certainly sold at a price we cannot pin down. A Best Offer listing that vanished quickly almost certainly sold; only its price is uncertain, and it belongs in the comp set with wider error bars. A listing found relisted under a new id almost certainly did not sell, and belongs out of the comp set entirely. Under one combined score both land around 0.6 to 0.7 and become indistinguishable. The same review also surfaced an outright bug that the conflation helped hide: the "ran to its scheduled end date, so nobody bought it" penalty was being applied to auctions, which end at their scheduled date by definition whether they sell or not, so every auction was penalised for behaving like an auction. An auction with twelve bids scored 0.324, below a listing about which nothing at all was known.

**Decision:** Return and store two independent scores. `sale_confidence` answers only whether a sale happened, and is driven by relist detection, bid count, scheduled-end behaviour for fixed-price listings, and disappearance speed. `price_confidence` answers only whether `price` plus `shipping_cost` reflects what was actually paid, and starts at 1.0 rather than at a hedge, because for a plain fixed-price listing with no offers the asking price simply *is* the price; it is then reduced for Best Offer (sold at an unknown discount), for auctions (the recorded figure is whatever bid was last observed, not the hammer price), and for unknown shipping. Because the two scores are now separate, each signal is applied to exactly one of them, and `accepts_best_offer` and `is_auction` no longer suppress the sale score, which they never had any business doing. Record the *direction* of price error in the signals where it is known, since it is not symmetric: an auction's last observed bid is a lower bound and so understates, while a Best Offer asking price overstates. Fix the auction end-date bug by making the scheduled-end signal apply only to non-auction listings.

**Alternatives considered:** Keeping one score and simply retuning the multipliers was rejected because the problem is structural rather than numerical: no single number can distinguish "probably did not happen" from "definitely happened at an unclear price", and stage 4 needs to act differently on each. Encoding the distinction in `sale_signals` alone, leaving one stored score, was rejected because it would force every consumer to re-derive the split by parsing JSON, which is exactly the work a typed column should do once. Emitting a single combined score alongside the two components was rejected as an attractive nuisance: it would be the easiest field to reach for and the one that reintroduces the conflation. Stage 4 should be made to think about which question it is asking.

**Consequences:** `Listing` gains `price_confidence`, and `sale_confidence` narrows in meaning, so scores written before this change are not comparable with the ones after; since no sale has ever been confirmed in production, nothing existing needs migrating. Stage 4 now has to consume both, and the intended shape is that `sale_confidence` gates whether a listing enters the comp set at all while `price_confidence` weights how much its price moves the estimate. The multipliers remain invented, and this decision does not improve their calibration; what it does is make them *separately* falsifiable, so that when real outcomes arrive the relist weight can be corrected without disturbing the Best Offer weight. Recording price-error direction opens a future option to correct a comp rather than merely discount it, which is deliberately not attempted here. The scores are ordinal and must not be read as probabilities; that was true before and is worth restating, because two numbers in the range [0, 1] sitting next to each other look even more like probabilities than one did.
